ProœmiumA Warning to the Reader

Reader, if you have found this book, it is because a book was needed. It does not matter how it came to you: whether it was in the effects of a dead aunt, or on a stall at a fair, or lying open on a table in a house you do not remember entering. It came. Books of this kind do.

Read it by day if you can. Do not read it aloud near open fields. Do not read it to children, though they will ask, for children always know when there is a story in the house that they are not being told.

And if, as you read, you should notice that the night outside has gone very quiet — close the book, and do not go to the window. It will not help you. But it is a kindness to yourself not to see.

You were warned. You read on. It is the same, every time.

Capitulum IOf the Silence That Goes Before

The miller’s boy at Hollin counted them by lantern. Four thousand and some, in the one acre. Every one with its head to the east.

1Before the light there is the silence; and before the silence there is nothing at all to warn you.

2It begins at the edge of a summer evening, in the hour when the fields are loudest. The crickets are at their sawing and the frogs at their bells, and the moths knock at the lamp-glass like small polite fists. You have heard this sound so long that you no longer hear it. That is the first cruelty: you will not know what you had until it is taken.

3Then it stops.

4Not dwindles. Not fades, as noise fades when the night cools. It stops, all of it, in one breath, the way a congregation falls silent when someone is standing in the doorway who should not be standing anywhere.

5The old ones say you can hear the silence arrive. It moves across the country like the shadow of a cloud, from field to field, and where it has passed the grass is full of insects that have not so much died as stopped: crickets with their legs drawn up, beetles on their backs, moths folded on the sills like letters no one will open. If you pick one up, it is warm. If you hold it to your ear you will hear, very faintly, the sound of a crowd.

Do not hold them to your ear.

6Mark this, for it is the only sign you will be given: the bugs go silent. Completely, totally silent. Not one wing. Not one leg drawn across another. And the dogs do not bark at it, as you would hope a dog would. The dogs lie down in the yards, all facing the same way, and wait.

7Three nights after the silence, the light comes.

It has gone quiet outside. Has it gone quiet where you are?

Capitulum IIOf Wellsee, Which Was a Town

1Hear now how it began, as it was told to me by a woman who was told it by her grandmother, who claimed to have been there, and who was not, I am assured, old enough to have been.

2There was a town called Wellsee, in a valley that is no longer on any map, and in the middle of the town there was a well. It was the town’s pride. It was older than the church and older than the oldest grave, and no bucket had ever found its bottom. The children dropped stones into it and counted, and gave up counting, and went home to supper with the stones still falling.

3In a dry year the people of Wellsee grew afraid for their water, and they resolved to learn how deep the well went. They lowered a lantern on a rope. When the rope ran out they tied on another, and another, until every rope in Wellsee had gone down into the dark and the lantern was a spark, and then the seed of a spark, and then a thing you could only see if you did not look at it straight.

Mark the name. Say it aloud, slowly, and hear what it says.

4It was the mayor’s youngest who said it first. Lower it again. We’ll see. And the people laughed, because that was the very phrase the town was named for, and they thought it was a joke.

5The rope went slack.

6A long while after — a week, some say; a generation, say others — the lantern came back up. No one was holding the rope. It rose out of the well on its own, swinging gently, as if carried by a careful hand, and it was still lit. But it was not lit by any oil of Wellsee. The light was the colour of a fever. It was warm the way a mouth is warm. And every one who looked at it found that they could not stop.

7That night the town of Wellsee folded up. The houses came down like tents. The church spire lay down in the street and was a centre pole. The square was stretched into a ring and sawdust was spread upon it; and the well, the well was at the very middle of the ring, beneath a lid of painted boards, where it remains. And every soul who had looked into the light took up a task — a hammer, or a rope, or a painted face — and they have not set it down since.

We’ll see. Well. See.

8This is why there is no town of Wellsee. The town did not build the carnival. The town is the carnival, folded up, and it has been travelling ever since, looking, I think, for more rope.

Capitulum IIIOf the Light, and How It Finds a Town

1On the third night after the silence a light appears where no light should be: over a hill that was dark the night before, in a field that was sown with barley, on the far side of a river that has no bridge.

2It is not a fire. Fires move. This light is steady, and it is many: strings of bulbs, gold as egg-yolk, draped from a peak that is taller than any tent ought to be. Under the bulbs, if the wind is right, there is music, a steam-organ playing a waltz that is very nearly one you know.

Asked? I did not ask. I do not remember asking.

3It does not come to towns at random. It comes where it is thought of. A child lies awake and wishes something would happen, anything at all. A widow thinks that she would give anything to feel something again. A drunk in a ditch tells the stars that nothing could be worse than this. It hears. The old books call this the Asking, and say that every town the carnival has visited had first, without knowing, asked.

4The light falls upon a town the way the eye of a man falls upon a coin in the road. Once it has found you there is no hiding. Shutters do not keep it out: it comes through the boards in thin gold lines and lies across the bed. Blindfolds do not help: the blind of Carrow Fen said they saw it plainer than any sighted man. It shines upon the face of every person in the town, one by one, slowly, as if reading them.

5And the moon, on those nights, is wrong. Every account agrees in this. It is too large, or too low, or the wrong colour; or there are two of it; or there is one, and it has turned, very slightly, to look at the tent.

Capitulum IVOf the Rabbits

1Of all the creatures of the field, the rabbits suffer it first, and worst, and longest.

2No one knows why. Some say it is because a rabbit is all appetite and fear, and the light feeds upon both. Some say it is because the first lantern, coming up out of the well, passed a warren in the stone. The keeper of the Hollin parish register wrote only: They were nearest to the ground, and the ground is where it lives.

3It begins with the eyes. A rabbit that has sat in the light has eyes like the bulbs: round, gold, and lit from behind. It stops eating. It sits upright at the edge of the field, very still, facing the tent, the way the dogs do.

Count the fingers.

4Then it grows. Not quickly. Night by night the legs lengthen, the hind legs most, until it can stand on them as a man stands; and it does stand, and it seems to find this a relief. The forepaws split into fingers. There are always too many fingers. The ears grow long, and then longer, and then begin to branch, like antlers, like the bare limbs of winter trees. The soft mouth draws back from teeth that were not there before — not a rabbit’s chisels but rows, many rows, as though the mouth were a theatre and the teeth its audience.

5They keep their fur. That is the worst of it. The fur stays soft, and grey-brown, and lovely, and the children of the towns are drawn to stroke it.

Fig. iv drawn from life. God forgive me, it held still for it.

6By the last night the rabbits are taller than the tent-flaps. They are the ones who take the tickets. They are the ones who stand at the edges of the midway, in the dark between the booths, and if you look at them they are always already looking at you. They do not speak. Their noses still twitch. One account, I will not say whose, swears that as he passed close, one of them whispered his name in his own mother’s voice.

7And when the carnival leaves, it does not take the rabbits. It leaves them in the fields. Ask the farmers in any valley it has passed through why they keep no rabbits; why they set wire and poison at every hedge; why they will not let their children walk to school alone across the long meadows. They will not answer you. But they will look toward the treeline.

Capitulum VOf the Men Who Screamed

1In the year the carnival came to Ashby Underhill there were eleven men in the town who swore that they would not go. They were sensible men: a doctor, a magistrate, a gunsmith, two brothers who farmed the high ground, and others whose names are in the register at the back of this book, where they are crossed through.

2They barred themselves in the magistrate’s house on the first night of the light, with their rifles and their Bibles and the doctor’s laudanum, and they agreed that they would sit with their backs to the windows and talk loudly of ordinary things until it was gone.

3On the second night, one of them began to scream.

4He did not scream from fear, said the doctor, who wrote it down. He screamed as a kettle screams: because something in him had been heated past bearing and must come out. And when the others went to hold him, they found that they had begun to scream too.

Eleven names. I have counted twelve voices.

5They screamed all that night. The doctor’s notes go on, in a hand that slants further across the page with every line. He writes that the screaming was not loud, at the end, but high and thin and continuous, like wind through a keyhole. He writes that it did not stop for breath. He writes that it could not, for there was nothing left to breathe with; that he put his ear to the gunsmith’s chest and heard the lungs folding, like wet paper, like a tent coming down. He writes that the mouths were still open, and still screaming, after.

6He writes, last of all, that the sound was going somewhere. That it went out between the shutters and across the fields toward the light, and that far off, under the bulbs, an organ took up the note and made it part of the waltz.

7The doctor’s notes end there. The magistrate’s house was found empty but for the rifles, still loaded, and the Bibles, every one of them lying open at the same page, which was blank. Of the eleven men nothing was ever found. But those who went to the carnival that year, and came home, said there was a new tent at the far end of the midway, where for a penny you might hear the Choir; and that the Choir did not breathe.

Capitulum VIOf Why They Still Come

1You will ask: knowing all this, why do they go? The rabbits, the Choir, the silent fields: the stories are not secret. Every grandmother knows them. Why does anyone walk toward the light?

2I answer: because it is the most beautiful thing they will ever see.

3Understand that the carnival does not frighten from a distance. From the hill it is the warmest thing in the world. The bulbs, the music, the smell of burnt sugar and hot oil and of something underneath them both that you remember from one morning of your childhood you have otherwise forgotten. It looks like every evening you were ever happy. It looks like being expected.

My sister went with a cup of tea in her hand. It was found in the lane, still warm. Not one drop spilled.

4And they do not decide to go. That is the second thing to understand. No one in all the old accounts ever describes deciding. A woman is drying a plate at her window, and then she is in the lane with the plate still in her hand. A man is reading to his children, and then he is at the gate with the book, and the children are beside him in their nightclothes, and none of them are cold.

5They walk in a line along the road, not hurrying, not speaking, each with their face turned up to the light the way a flower turns to the sun. And their shadows stretch out long behind them, back toward the homes they have left, back toward the doors still open and the lamps still lit — the shadows reaching back, though the people do not.

6And still people come. Every summer, in some valley, they come.

Capitulum VIIOf Why One Does Not Refuse

1There have been those who refused. The legend keeps their names so that you will understand what refusal costs.

2Margery Kell of Saltmarsh nailed shut her door and her windows and sat with her back against them for three nights while the light came through the cracks. On the fourth morning, when the carnival had gone, her neighbours broke in and found her sitting just so, alive and well, and she rose and thanked them and went about her business.

3But she cast no shadow after that. And she never again slept. And if you asked her, she would tell you, very politely, that she was waiting for the light to come back; that she could hear it always, just over the hill, the waltz, very nearly finished. She lived to be one hundred and four. She was waiting all of it.

Margery Kell was my grandmother’s name. I did not know it until I had written it.

4The carnival does not punish refusal. It does something worse. It remembers you. It goes away and leaves you here, in a world where the crickets have started singing again, and it keeps the part of you that looked at the light. And every summer evening for the rest of your life you will feel the place where that part was, like the gap of a pulled tooth, and you will listen at the window for the silence.

5That is why one does not refuse. Not because one cannot. Because those who did, mostly, went looking for it afterwards: on foot, in the wrong direction, in their nightclothes, asking strangers on the road whether they had heard the bugs stop. And the carnival does not return for those who have refused it once.

6Mostly.

Capitulum VIIIOf the Fates Worse Than Death

1What becomes of those who go in? Here my sources fail me, and I must give you what I have, which is not much, and which I wish were less.

2Some come home. They come home in the grey of the morning with sawdust on their shoes and a paper prize in their hands, and they are themselves in every way, and their families weep for gladness. And then, over the weeks that follow, the families notice that the one who came home smiles a little too long; that he laughs at nothing in the next room, when he is alone; that his face, at certain angles, in candlelight, seems to have been painted on.

3Some do not come home, and are seen again, years later and very far away, in the carnival itself: behind a counter, at the foot of a ride, in the ring. ExplicitHere the Manuscript Ends

This book was found on a kitchen table at the edge of a barley field, the ink of its last line still wet, beside a cold cup of tea and an open window. Outside, the bugs were silent. Of the scribe there was no further trace, unless you count the shadow, which the farmer’s wife swore was still on the wall above the chair, and which she painted over twice.
