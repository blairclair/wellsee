State Bureau of Investigation · Missing Persons Division

The Unwilling

File 31-W · Disappearances on “Light Nights” · 1931–1987

Do not refile

Don't call them clowns. Clowns choose it. — M.L.

Every town that has seen the Wellsee light tells you the same thing first: you go. When the glow comes up over the ridge and the crickets stop, you put on your shoes and you walk toward it with your neighbours, and you do not argue. What happens to the people who go is another story, and other people tell it.

This file is about the ones who stayed home.

It was assembled over twenty-three years by Investigator Margaret Lund of the State Bureau, who began it as a routine consolidation of missing-persons reports and could not, afterward, put it down. Her typed reports are reproduced as she filed them. Her handwriting — in red where she went back over old pages, in pencil where she was in a hurry — is reproduced as she left it. The photographs are described from the originals, which are not available for viewing.

Read it in order. She did.

  1. 14 Oct 1931Pinhook Hollow
    County Sheriff · Report of Death & Absence31-W-001

    Tobias Fenn, 41, dairyman

    Absent
    Tobias Fenn, husband
    Deceased
    Mary Ellen Fenn, 38 — consumption, natural causes
    Last seen
    Evening of 13 Oct., at his own front gate, by neighbours walking to the light

    Tobias Fenn married Mary Ellen Cobb in the spring of 1912 and was, by every account in the Hollow, a little foolish about her for the rest of his life. He sang badly in church so she would have something to laugh at during the sermon. When the consumption took her legs in 1929 he sold half the herd to buy a wheeled chair from Kansas City, and when it took her strength for the chair he carried her out to the porch every evening anyway, so she could watch the cows come down.

    On the night of the thirteenth the light came up behind Shoal Ridge. The Purcells stopped at the Fenn gate on their way and offered to help carry her. Mrs. Purcell gave this statement:

    He said, “She can't walk to it, and I won't carry her to it. You go on.” He wasn't angry. He thanked us. He said he was going to brush her hair. We could hear the bugs had gone quiet and Ida was crying, so we went on.

    At first light the Purcells found the front door open. Mary Fenn was in her bed, dead some hours, her hands folded and her hair washed and done up in a long braid, which she had not been able to lift her arms to do in a year. A cup of water had been set where she could reach it. The chair beside the bed was empty. Its arms were marked with white grease, in the shape of two hands, as if someone had sat there a long time holding on.

    Tobias Fenn did not come home.

    M.L., 1959 — Who braided her hair? She could not lift her arms. Look at the window. I looked at this photograph for seven years before I saw it.

  2. 3 May 1938Varner’s Ford
    Missing Person · Statement Summary31-W-006

    Lettie Ames, 26, schoolteacher

    Miss Ames taught all eight grades of the Varner’s Ford school in a single room, and taught them, by the testimony of every parent the county interviewed, as if each one of them were going to be somebody. She bought chalk out of her own wages. She kept a jar of horehound drops for the ones who came without breakfast and never once asked why. She was engaged to Carl Dewey, who carried the rural mail, and they were to be married in June, when school let out.

    On the evening of the second, nineteen children were at the schoolhouse past dark, rehearsing the spring pageant. The light came up over the river at about nine o’clock.

    Parents walking toward it assumed Miss Ames would bring the children along. She did not. She barred the door. She turned every desk in the room to face the walls, so that no child could see the windows, and she sat in the middle of the floor with a lamp and read to them from The Wind in the Willows until they fell asleep, one by one. Wilma Tate, aged nine, was the last awake. Her statement, taken by her mother:

    Miss Ames said we were not going anywhere tonight and that was her decision and not ours, so nobody would be in trouble. She did the voices for Toad. Then somebody knocked and she said, “Put your heads down, class.” I put my head down. She said, “Good girl, Wilma.” She didn't sound scared. She sounded like when the inspector came.

    The children were found at sunrise, every one of them asleep and unharmed, heads down on their desks. The bar was still across the door. On the blackboard, in Miss Ames’s round copybook hand, was the morning’s lesson.

    M.L., 1962 — Barred from the inside. Then how did she leave? Remember the chalk.

  3. 19 Nov 1938Varner’s Ford Courier
    Newspaper Clipping · Attached31-W-006-C

    “Postman Keeps His Route to Empty School”

    Six months after the disappearance of schoolmistress Miss Lettie Ames, rural carrier Carl Dewey, her intended, continues to stop his mail wagon each afternoon at the Varner's Ford schoolhouse, now closed, and to leave a letter at the door. "She'll want to know what's been happening," Mr. Dewey told this paper. "She always wanted to know what was happening. I tell her about the children mostly. Wilma Tate is reading Dickens now." Asked whether he had gone to the light on the night in question, Mr. Dewey said he had, and that he was sorry for it, and that he did not wish to speak further.

  4. 31 Aug 1946Dunmore
    Witness Statement · Mrs. June Hale31-W-014

    Samuel Hale, 24, returned serviceman

    Samuel Hale came home from the Pacific in March of 1946 thinner, quieter, and unable to sleep with a door closed. He married June Albright, whom he had written to every week of the war, on the twenty-fourth of August. They had been married six days. The following is taken from Mrs. Hale’s statement, given in 1951 to Investigator Lund, who noted that the witness asked twice to stop and both times asked to continue.

    Sam said he'd walked toward enough lights. That's what he called it. He said the army had marched him toward every light on every island they could find and he was done going where he was told.

    I wanted to stay with him. I want you to write that down. I wanted to stay. He held my face in his hands and said, “Junie, I know what it is to be sent. I'm sending you. You go with the Morrows and you don't look back, and in the morning I'll make you breakfast and I'll be ashamed of myself.” He made me promise on my ring.

    So I went. I won't tell you what I saw there. I came home at sunup and the house was boarded. Every window, and the front door. The nails were on the outside. His tags were hanging on the doorknob.

    I've thought about it for five years. If he'd been inside, keeping something out, the nails would be on the inside. He was outside. He boarded it up after himself. I think he was keeping something from coming back in to me. I think he was keeping himself out.

    M.L., 1959 — She was right. He was keeping himself out. That means there was still that much of him left, that first night. I need that to be true.

  5. 12 Mar 1951State Bureau, Room 4
    Investigator’s Notes · M. Lund31-W

    Assigned today to consolidate “Light Night absences,” six counties, 1931 to present. Director Pryor says it is a filing job. Forty-one names.

    Every town says the same thing. Nobody who walked toward the light has been reported missing. Not one. They come home at dawn — changed, some of them, and they won't say how — but they come home. Every one of the forty-one stayed home. A farmer with a dying wife. A teacher with nineteen children. A soldier on his honeymoon. Not cowards. Not one of them was a coward.

    Nora asked at supper why I was quiet. Told her it was a filing job.

  6. 10 Oct 1953Hollis
    Missing Juvenile · Investigator’s Report31-W-044

    Eli Pruitt, 11, and Biscuit

    Reporting investigator: M. Lund. Attended in person, 10 Oct., 11:00 a.m.

    Eli Pruitt was eleven years old and small for it, freckled, with a cowlick his mother had given up on. He collected cicada shells in a cigar box — there were two hundred and six of them, each labelled with the date it was found. His dog was a yellow mutt named Biscuit, who had come to the farm as a stray during a thunderstorm and remained afraid of loud noises, bright lights, and being alone, in that order.

    When the light rose over the Pruitt fields on the night of the ninth, Biscuit went under the corn crib and would not come out. Mrs. Pruitt had the two little girls by the hand and called for Eli to come on. Eli called back that Biscuit was scared, and that he would bring her in a minute, and that his mother should go ahead. His mother, by her own account, went ahead. She said to me: “Everybody says you go. Everybody says. So I went. I had the girls.”

    In the morning the field behind the crib was empty except for Biscuit, sitting upright in the stubble, facing east. Beside her were Eli’s boots, set side by side, the laces tied together in a bow the way he tied them to hang them on a nail. The dog would not leave the spot. She was carried home twice and was back in the field both times by noon. Neighbours left water for her there for nine days.

    The insects did not return to the Pruitt farm that autumn. Mrs. Pruitt says she noticed because Eli would have.

    M.L., 1962 — Dorothy Kemp's terrier. See below. God forgive me, see below.

    I bought Biscuit a collar. I don't know why I'm putting that in an official file.

  7. 20 Jul 1958Carrow Falls
    Coroner’s Inquest & Recovered Film31-W-061

    The Benning house

    Arthur Benning, 33, a pharmacist’s clerk and an amateur photographer of birds, refused to go to the light on the night of the nineteenth. He had read the newspaper accounts of the absences; he had, he told his brothers, a theory. His brothers Lyle and Owen Benning and two county deputies, Hayes and Kortright, agreed to stand watch at the house with him so that whatever came for the ones who stayed behind could be seen, and stopped. Arthur set up his camera on a tripod at the parlour window, with a flash, and waited.

    At dawn the Benning house was found with every window open. Arthur was gone. His camera was still on the tripod. The four men who had stood watch for him were in the front yard.

    FINDINGS (abridged). Four adult males, deceased. Cause of death in each: bilateral collapse of the lungs. No penetrating wound. The coroner notes that such collapse is consistent with sustained forced exhalation over a long period: "as though each man screamed until there was nothing left in him to scream with." Jaws fixed open. Over each open mouth a second mouth had been painted, in red grease, wide and upturned. The faces had been whitened. Paint was applied before death. Neighbours at half a mile report screaming from about 11 p.m. until a little before 4. Also laughter. Mrs. D. Orr: "Not like people laugh. Like something that learned it off a phonograph." Insects absent from the property for three nights following.

    The film in Arthur Benning’s camera had one exposure on it.

    M.L., 22 Jul. 1958 — The tall one is wearing overalls and there are two white handprints on the bib where he has wiped his hands. The woman's fingers are white to the second knuckle. Chalk. The young one has two discs on a chain. The little one has no shoes.

    I know them. I know every one of them. Tobias. Lettie. Sam. Eli. Go back and look at the window in the Fenn photograph. It was always there.

  8. 6 Jan 1959Memorandum to Director
    Memorandum · Not Sent31-W

    Sir —

    They are not victims who became monsters. They are victims, and they are monsters, and the one does not cancel the other. I have to hold both or I can't do this job.

    Here is what the file shows. The carnival does not keep the people who walk to it. It keeps the people who don't. The ones who refuse are taken in the night, and they are made over — painted, emptied, given that laugh — and then, the next time the light rises somewhere, they are sent out to fetch the next one who refuses. Every name in this file was fetched by a name earlier in this file. Arthur Benning will fetch someone. It is a chain, and it only goes one direction.

    And anyone who stands in the doorway between them and the one they've come for dies in the doorway, the way Lyle and Owen Benning did. Screaming. For hours. With the faces of people we loved bent over them, smiling.

    Tobias Fenn sat with his wife until she died. Now he sits with others. I have found him, I believe, in four more files: always beside a bed, always holding on to the arms of the chair. Lettie Ames writes the names of the next ones on schoolhouse blackboards a week before the light comes. Carrow Falls school, 12 July 1958: Arthur. Lyle. Owen. Dale. Henry. Nobody knew what it meant. Samuel Hale nails the doors shut from the outside — not to keep them out now. To keep the rest of us out.

    And Eli asks for dogs.

    I cried for Eli Pruitt in 1953. I have his shoe size in my files. Mrs. Orr saw them carry Owen Benning out into the yard, still screaming. She said the little one had his feet, and was skipping.

  9. 3 Jun 1962Esterly
    Witness Statement · Juvenile31-W-072

    Dorothy Kemp, 8

    Dorothy Kemp’s family went to the light on the night of the second. Dorothy went with them. This statement concerns the night of 26 May, one week earlier, when there was no light, and she woke at about two in the morning. Taken by M. Lund in the presence of her mother.

    There was a boy at my window. He was all white like a cake, and his mouth was painted on bigger than his mouth. He had his hand on the glass. He wasn't scary at first because he was littler than me.

    He said could he please have Pepper. I said no. He said he'd be really nice to her. He said he used to have a dog and she was yellow and she was scared of everything and he was the only one who could make her not be scared. He said he couldn't remember her name. He asked did I know it. He was crying but he was smiling too. Then he started laughing and he didn't stop and I hid under the covers.

    When I came out from under, it was quiet. You know how in summer it's loud outside at night? It wasn't loud.

    On the night of the light, the Kemps took their terrier, Pepper, with them on a lead. Somewhere in the walk across the Halvorsen pasture the lead went slack. Mrs. Kemp has the collar. It is still buckled.

    He couldn't remember her name. He asked a child if she knew it. Her name was Biscuit, Eli. Her name was Biscuit.

  10. 15 Apr 1967Varner’s Ford
    Missing Person · Note Recovered31-W-088

    Carl Dewey, 55, rural carrier (retired)

    The light came back to Varner’s Ford for the first time in twenty-nine years on the night of the fourteenth. Carl Dewey had retired from the post office in January. Neighbours saw his porch lamp burning all night. They did not stop to ask him to come. By then, in Varner’s Ford, people knew better than to stop.

    In the morning his mailbag was found on the step of the old schoolhouse. It was empty. Every one of his letters — there were over seven thousand, by the postmistress’s estimate, twenty-nine years of them — had been taken from the place he’d been keeping them and pushed, one by one, into the gaps around the schoolhouse door, the window frames, the loose clapboards, until the building bristled with paper. His cap was on the rail. A note was left on his kitchen table, addressed to no one:

    I went to the light in ’38 because everybody said you go. I have been sorry every day since. I know what I'm choosing. If she's there, then I would rather be there than here. Somebody please feed the cat.

    He is the only one in this file who went to them on purpose, for love. They took him the same as the others. Love does not get you a better door.

Investigator Lund is reassigned to Records, effective immediately. Further inquiry into so-called "Light Nights" will only increase the number of is not in the public interest. Personnel are reminded that in the event of the light any civil emergency, the Bureau's guidance remains GO. GO WITH YOUR FAMILIES. DO NOT to follow the direction of local authorities. Investigator Lund's personal materials, photographs and correspondence relating to 31-W are to be surrendered by 15 Sept.

    Director Pryor went to the light in '64 with his wife and both his boys. Of course he wants it closed. He knows which door he walked through. He wants to keep believing there was a choice and he made the right one.

    There's no right one. Go, and you see what you see there, and you come home with it in you for the rest of your life. Stay, and they come for you, and you become them. Stand in the door for someone you love, and you die in the door. That's all the doors. I've checked every one. I have twenty years of checking.

  14. TodayState Archive, Box 31
    Archivist’s Addendum31-W-131

    An uncatalogued card

    File 31-W was transferred to the State Archive in 1988 and has been requested four times since. When the box was opened for this transcription, one item was found that does not appear on any inventory: an unlined index card, white with chalk dust, lying on top of the folder as though it had just been set down.

    It is written in a round copybook hand that matches the Varner’s Ford blackboard of 1938. It is dated in the same hand. The date is today’s.

We have tried, in every way available to an archive, to find a version of this file where somebody gets out. We have not found it.

If you go when the light comes, you will see what you see there, and you will carry it home in you for the rest of your life, and you will never say what it was.

If you refuse, they will come for you — the people this file has made you love — and they will be gentle, and they will be laughing, and in the morning you will be one of them, and the next time the light rises you will go and fetch someone else who said no.

If you stand in the door for someone you love, you will die in the door, screaming, with a painted mouth over your own.

That is all the doors. Margaret Lund checked every one of them for twenty-three years, and then she checked the last one herself.

So when it comes for your town — and it finds every town, in the end — put on your shoes. Walk toward it with your neighbours. Do not argue. It will not be better, what you find. It will only be not this.

One does not refuse the carnival.

Before you close this page: listen. Are the insects still singing where you are?
